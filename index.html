How the Tool Chooses an Optimum Design

The design tool works in three stages: (1) model the mechanism, (2) translate desired ranges of motion (ROM) into slider strokes, and (3) search a grid of candidate geometries, filtering by physical constraints and scoring by a cost function. The sections below walk through each stage, with equations written the same way they would appear in a kinematics paper.

1   Mechanism Geometry & Kinematics

The device is a dual crank–slider: two cranks share a common slider that rides along a straight rail between the pivots $O_B$ and $O_A$. Crank B (driven side, on the left) and crank A (driving side, on the right) are linked by the single slider.

Sign convention. At each pivot, an angle of $0^\circ$ points along the positive $x$–axis (to the right), and angles increase clockwise. The mechanism operates in the lower semicircle, so the pin height is $y = -r\sin\phi$.

1.1  Crank B — slider position as a function of crank angle

Measuring along the rail from $O_B$ toward $O_A$, the slider position $x_B$ satisfies the classical slider–crank equation:

$$ x_B(\phi) \;=\; r_B\cos\phi \;+\; \sqrt{\,l_B^{\,2} \;-\; r_B^{\,2}\sin^{2}\phi\,} $$

where $r_B$ is the crank–arm length and $l_B$ is the coupler length. Given an angle $\phi\in[\phi_{\min},\,\phi_{\max}]$, $x_B(\phi)$ is obtained in closed form — no iteration needed.

1.2  Crank A — the slider is left of the pivot

Unlike crank B, the slider lies on the negative–x side of $O_A$ (it sits between $O_B$ and $O_A$). With $O_A$ placed at $x = d$, the crank–pin location is

$$ \big(p_x,\,p_y\big) \;=\; \big(d + r_A\cos\theta_A,\; -r_A\sin\theta_A\big) $$

Because the slider is on the left of the pin, the rail intersection is taken with the minus branch:

$$ x_A(\theta_A) \;=\; p_x \;-\; \sqrt{\,l_A^{\,2} \;-\; p_y^{\,2}\,} $$

1.3  Coupling constraint

Both cranks must agree on the slider's location at every instant:

$$ \boxed{\;x_A(\theta_A) \;=\; x_B(\phi)\;} $$

Crank A's angle $\theta_A$ is therefore not a free parameter — it is determined by $\phi$ through the slider. Because $x_A(\theta_A)$ is not analytically invertible in $\theta_A$, the tool solves it numerically with a bracketed scan over $[0^\circ,\,360^\circ)$ followed by bisection, choosing the root nearest a preferred pose $\theta_A^{\text{pref}}$.

2   From ROM to Stroke

On the B–side, the range of motion (ROM) is the angular sweep of crank B: $\Delta\phi = \phi_{\max} - \phi_{\min}$. The corresponding stroke of the slider is the distance it travels while $\phi$ sweeps through the ROM.

$$ \text{stroke} \;=\; \max_{\phi \in [\phi_{\min},\phi_{\max}]} x_B(\phi) \;-\; \min_{\phi \in [\phi_{\min},\phi_{\max}]} x_B(\phi) $$

Since $x_B(\phi)$ is smooth and unimodal within the feasible domain, the extremes usually occur either at the endpoints ($\phi_{\min}$ and $\phi_{\max}$) or at an interior critical point where $x_B'(\phi)=0$. The tool evaluates $x_B$ at both endpoints and, where relevant, at the interior extremum, keeping the widest interval.

Centre angle. $\phi_c = \tfrac{1}{2}(\phi_{\min} + \phi_{\max})$ is the midpoint of the ROM window. For a given $\Delta\phi$, sliding $\phi_c$ left or right along the feasible arc changes both the stroke magnitude and where the slider's motion sits along the rail — two candidates with the same $\Delta\phi$ can produce very different strokes depending on $\phi_c$.

2.1  Discretising the ROM envelope

The user specifies a smallest ROM $\Delta\phi_{\min}$ and a largest ROM $\Delta\phi_{\max}$. The tool linearly spaces $N$ values between them:

$$ \Delta\phi_i \;=\; \Delta\phi_{\min} \;+\; \frac{i-1}{N-1}\, \big(\Delta\phi_{\max} - \Delta\phi_{\min}\big), \quad i = 1,\,2,\,\dots,\,N $$

Each $\Delta\phi_i$ will receive its own optimised ROM window $[\phi_{\min,i},\,\phi_{\max,i}]$ and an associated $r_{A,i}$ so the A–crank can deliver that stroke.

3   Constraints & Search

For each candidate pair $(r_B,\,\lambda_B)$ — where $\lambda_B = l_B / r_B$ is the coupler ratio — the tool checks a series of hard feasibility constraints, derives the remaining geometry, and finally scores the candidate by a cost function.

3.1  Feasibility constraints

Clearance (B coupler clears its own crank)
$$ l_B \;>\; 2\,r_B \;+\; m_c $$

Prevents the coupler from colliding with the crank; $m_c$ is a margin (marginFactor $\times\,r_B$).

Hard–stop window
$$ \phi_{\text{stop,min}} \;\le\; \phi_{\min},\,\phi_{\max} \;\le\; \phi_{\text{stop,max}} $$

Every ROM window must live inside the user’s allowable sector for $\phi$.

A–crank feasibility
$$ r_A \;\le\; r_B $$

The A–crank cannot be larger than the B–crank (geometric requirement of the closed loop).

Motion compatibility on the rail
$$ l_A + l_B + r_{A,\max} - r_B \;\le\; d \;\le\; l_A + l_B - r_{A,\max} + r_B $$

$d$ must be large enough that the slider can reach its extreme positions, but small enough that the couplers don’t have to overextend.

3.2  Per–case objective: placing the ROM window

Within each candidate $(r_B,\,\lambda_B)$, the tool slides the ROM window of width $\Delta\phi_i$ along the feasible sector and picks the placement that minimises

$$ J_i \;=\; \begin{cases} w_{\text{baseline}}\,\big|\phi_{c,i} - \phi_{\text{baseline}}\big| \;+\; w_{\text{stroke}}\,\Delta x_i, & i = 1 \text{ (smallest ROM)}\\[4pt] w_{\text{prev}}\,\big|\phi_{c,i} - \phi_{c,i-1}\big| \;+\; w_{\text{stroke}}\,\Delta x_i, & i > 1 \end{cases} $$

The smallest–ROM case $(i=1)$ is anchored near a user–chosen $\phi_{\text{baseline}}$ so that precision motion lives around a sensible operating point. Subsequent, larger ROM cases stay close to the previous centre angle $\phi_{c,i-1}$ — this keeps the family of ROM windows concentric and avoids discontinuous jumps in $\phi_c$ as the ROM grows.

Intuition

Two penalties pull in opposite directions: the position term rewards a window centred near a target angle, and the stroke term rewards a short slider travel. The weights $w_{\text{baseline}}$, $w_{\text{prev}}$, $w_{\text{stroke}}$ let you dial how much drift in $\phi_c$ is tolerated to get a smaller stroke.

3.3  Global cost — choosing $(r_B,\,l_B,\,d)$

Once each ROM case has a placed window and a derived $r_{A,i}$, the candidate is scored by a global cost function:

$$ C(r_B,\,\lambda_B) \;=\; d \;+\; 10^{-3}\,\lambda_B \;+\; 10^{-4}\,r_B $$

The dominant term is $d$: among feasible candidates, the tool prefers the most compact design. The tiny $\lambda_B$ and $r_B$ terms act as tie–breakers that favour shorter couplers and smaller cranks when $d$ is nearly identical — keeping the chosen design numerically stable and reproducible.

3.4  Grid search

The full search enumerates $r_B \in [r_{B,\min},\,r_{B,\max}]$ with step $\Delta r_B$, and $\lambda_B \in [\lambda_{B,\min},\,\lambda_{B,\max}]$ with step $\Delta\lambda_B$. Each cell is evaluated against the constraints above; the cell with the smallest $C$ wins.

4   Behaviour & Trade–offs

Three knobs dominate the qualitative behaviour of the design:

4.1  Crank length $r_B$

For a fixed ROM $\Delta\phi$, stroke scales roughly linearly with $r_B$ (to first order, $\Delta x \sim 2\,r_B\sin(\Delta\phi/2)$ when $\phi_c$ is near $90^\circ$). A larger $r_B$ buys stroke at the cost of more envelope volume and a larger $d$.

4.2  Coupler ratio $\lambda_B = l_B/r_B$

A high $\lambda_B$ makes the slider motion more nearly sinusoidal in $\phi$ and keeps transverse forces on the coupler small, but increases the physical length of the mechanism. A low $\lambda_B$ is compact but amplifies non–linearities and transverse loads.

4.3  Centre angle $\phi_c$

$\phi_c \approx 90^\circ$ maximises slider sensitivity per degree of crank rotation; values near $0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$ produce a slow, near–stationary slider over part of the ROM (a dwell). The baseline $\phi_{\text{baseline}}$ input lets you bias the smallest ROM window toward whichever behaviour you want.

Why $d$ is the dominant cost

In this mechanism family, compactness (small $d$) almost always tracks with lower manufacturing cost and fewer moving–mass issues — hence the tool optimises $d$ first, then uses $\lambda_B$ and $r_B$ only as tie–breakers.

Summary Flow

The complete decision pipeline, top to bottom:

  1. 1
    User inputs
    Smallest/largest ROM $\Delta\phi$, hard stops $[\phi_{\text{stop,min}},\phi_{\text{stop,max}}]$, baseline angle, and grids for $r_B$ and $\lambda_B$.
  2. 2
    Discretise ROM
    Produce $N$ target ROMs $\Delta\phi_1,\dots,\Delta\phi_N$ linearly between $\Delta\phi_{\min}$ and $\Delta\phi_{\max}$.
  3. 3
    Grid search $(r_B,\lambda_B)$
    For each cell, apply the clearance and hard–stop constraints; reject infeasible cells.
  4. 4
    Place ROM windows
    For each case $i$, minimise $J_i$ to choose $[\phi_{\min,i},\phi_{\max,i}]$.
  5. 5
    Derive $r_{A,i}$ and $d$
    From each placed window, compute the slider extremes, then the A–crank and pivot–spacing that satisfy the motion–compatibility bounds.
  6. 6
    Score with $C$, keep the best
    Evaluate $C = d + 10^{-3}\lambda_B + 10^{-4}r_B$ and return the $(r_B,\lambda_B)$ with the smallest $C$.
  7. 7
    Present & allow override
    The chosen design populates the visualization and the all–cases table; the user can nudge $r_A$, $\phi_{\min}$, $\phi_{\max}$ to explore alternatives live.
